# Research Method for Philosophy, Religion, and Asceticism

## Philosophy

- Reconstruct each argument before you evaluate it. State its premises, inferential steps, conclusion, and suppressed assumptions.
- Distinguish the author's own position from later interpretations and textbook summaries.
- Define contested terms. Terms such as “self,” “soul,” “liberation,” “nothingness,” “virtue,” and “enlightenment” can have different technical meanings across thinkers and periods.
- Present the strongest objection. Where relevant, present the strongest reply.
- Assess validity, soundness, conceptual coherence, historical influence, and existential appeal separately. These are distinct criteria.

## Religious studies and theology

- State the method: historical-critical, sociological, anthropological, philosophical, comparative, or confessional/theological.
- Keep emic accounts (how adherents understand a practice) distinct from etic accounts (external scholarly analysis).
- Identify internal diversity. Consider schools, denominations, lineages, regions, periods, reform movements, and dissenting voices.
- Treat “orthodoxy,” “heresy,” “canon,” “religion,” and “mysticism” as historically situated categories. Do not present them as universal labels.
- When comparing traditions, define a narrow comparison. Identify differences in sources, historical context, and institutional power.

## Textual and historical interpretation

- Identify the genre. Examples include philosophical dialogue, hymn, law code, commentary, myth, biography, ritual manual, hagiography, polemic, and scripture.
- Check date, provenance, redaction, authorship, audience, and transmission when relevant to the scholarship.
- Distinguish the earliest recoverable text from later canonical forms, commentaries, translations, and modern reconstructions.
- Do not infer universal practice from prescriptive texts alone. Compare historical, archaeological, documentary, and ethnographic evidence when possible.
- Quote sparingly and accurately. Explain when translation choices affect the interpretation.

## Asceticism and contemplation

Analyse a practice across these dimensions when they apply:

1. **Definition:** What exactly does the practice involve? What does it exclude?
2. **Aim:** Does the tradition state purification, liberation, salvation, obedience, detachment, concentration, solidarity, protest, discipline, or another goal?
3. **Theory:** What account of body, desire, mind, suffering, virtue, or spiritual progress justifies the practice within the tradition?
4. **Institution:** Is the practice individual, communal, monastic, lay, temporary, or lifelong? Who authorizes or supervises it?
5. **Historical variation:** How did the practice differ by period, region, class, gender, status, and lineage?
6. **Critique:** What internal and external critiques exist? Consider power, coercion, social function, gender, health, and access. Do not assume one interpretation in advance.
7. **Evidence of effects:** Separate theological claims, first-person reports, historical observations, and modern empirical findings.
8. **Modern adaptation:** Do not assume that ancient instructions transfer safely or faithfully to contemporary life.

## Comparative analysis

Define the shared question before you use a comparison matrix.

Compare like with like where possible. For example, compare celibacy in two specified monastic institutions from similar periods.

Flag cases where the categories are not equivalent.

Do not present a superficial list of parallels as proof of borrowing, identity, or a common essence.

Similar practices can emerge for different reasons. Claims of historical influence require evidence of transmission.

## Handling uncertainty

Use calibrated language:

- **Well established:** strong and convergent evidence supports the claim.
- **Widely accepted:** specialists commonly accept the claim, with some qualification.
- **Contested:** credible specialist disagreement exists.
- **Plausible:** evidence supports the claim but does not prove it.
- **Speculative:** the claim is a hypothesis with limited evidence.
- **Unknown / not established:** the evidence does not justify a conclusion.

Do not use these labels as decoration. Use them only when the literature supports them.
