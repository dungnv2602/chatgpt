## Scene Text

On Tuesday evening, I asked Maya if she would like to walk with me on Saturday.

I had written the message twice. The first version included an explanation of the route, which made the invitation sound like a guided tour. The second was shorter.

*There's a route I usually take on Saturday mornings. Would you like to walk it with me this weekend?*

Her reply came a few minutes later.

*I'd like that. Is there anything I should know?*

*Comfortable shoes. No dramatic scenery.*

*I can manage one of those.*

I read the message again, smiling before I could decide whether it deserved the reaction.

On Saturday, I met her near the corner where the route began. It was not a place anyone would have chosen as a destination. There was a bus stop, a shuttered repair shop, and a row of narrow buildings whose ground floors had been given over to practical businesses.

“You weren't exaggerating about the scenery,” Maya said.

“I thought it best to establish expectations.”

“Very considerate.”

We started down the street. I had walked it often enough to know which paving stones shifted underfoot and where the pavement narrowed around a tree. I usually came this way alone, without needing to decide where to go next.

“Do you always walk this way?” she asked.

“Most Saturdays.”

“Why?”

I considered giving her the practical answer. It was quiet in the morning. The traffic was manageable. There was a shop I liked farther along. All true, and none of it quite sufficient.

“Because I know it,” I said. “I don't have to spend the walk deciding where I'm going.”

“That sounds restful.”

“It is. You start noticing other things.”

“Such as?”

“The same window being open. A new plant on a balcony. Whether the bakery has put the chairs outside.”

Maya looked along the row of buildings. “So the route changes, even when you don't.”

“Occasionally. I try not to take it personally.”

She laughed, and we continued.

The stationery shop was halfway down the next street. It was narrow, with shelves of notebooks and envelopes arranged behind the counter. A bell sounded when we entered.

The shopkeeper looked up.

“Your usual?”

“Please.”

Maya glanced at me.

“Do you have a usual notebook?” she asked.

“I have a usual size.”

“That's different.”

“It's a distinction of some importance.”

The shopkeeper brought out a plain notebook with a dark cover. I checked the paper, turned a few pages, and nodded. It was the kind I preferred: uncoated, slightly warm in color, with enough weight that ink did not show through too easily.

“You write in them?” Maya asked.

“Sometimes.”

“What do you write?”

“Things I don't want to forget.”

She waited, but I didn't elaborate. It was not a secret. I simply hadn't decided how to describe it. A sentence overheard on a train. A phrase from a manuscript. A list of errands. An observation that seemed worth keeping for no reason I could defend.

“Important things?” she asked.

“Not necessarily.”

“Then why keep them?”

“I suppose I like having somewhere to put them.”

She considered that, then picked up a small notebook from the display. Its cover was a muted green.

“This one looks like it would contain better ideas,” she said.

“An ambitious notebook.”

“Perhaps it has high standards.”

She returned it to the shelf without buying it. I paid for mine, and we went back outside.

The bakery was a little farther along. It was not especially attractive, but the bread was good and the tables near the window were usually free before noon. I had stopped there often enough that the attendant recognized me, though not well enough to know anything about my life.

“Two coffees?” I asked Maya.

“Tea, if they have it.”

“They do.”

I ordered, then hesitated at the counter. I usually took the same small pastry, but there was another one in the case that I had not tried.

Maya noticed.

“You can get the unfamiliar one,” she said.

“I wasn't aware there was a test.”

“There isn't. I'm simply observing.”

I chose the unfamiliar pastry. We carried our drinks to a table near the window.

For a while, we talked about ordinary things: a book Maya had started and abandoned, a client who had changed their mind about a path after it had already been laid, a manuscript I had spent most of Thursday trying to make less determined to explain itself.

Then Maya looked at the notebook beside my cup.

“Do you bring that everywhere?”

“Not everywhere.”

“Only on important Saturdays?”

“Only when I expect to be asked difficult questions.”

“Then I should have prepared better.”

“You've done very well.”

She smiled and looked out of the window. A delivery rider stopped outside, checked a message, and continued. Across the street, someone was trying to coax a reluctant dog through a doorway.

“It's a good route,” she said.

“Even without dramatic scenery?”

“Especially without it, perhaps. You know where you're going, but you still notice what's there.”

I turned the notebook slightly so its edge lined up with the table.

“I wanted to show you somewhere I come back to,” I said. “Not because there's anything remarkable about it. It's just part of my week.”

Maya looked at me, then at the street beyond the glass.

“I like that you showed me.”

The answer was simple. I had no need to improve it.

When we finished our drinks, I glanced at the time. There was no reason we had to leave immediately. I could have said I needed to get home, or that I had errands to run. Both would have been plausible.

“Would you like to sit a little longer?” I asked.

Maya settled back in her chair.

“I would.”

Outside, the dog had finally made it through the doorway. I watched it disappear from view, then turned back to the table.

For once, I had not waited for someone else to decide where the morning should go.
