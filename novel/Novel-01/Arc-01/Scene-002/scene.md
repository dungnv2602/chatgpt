## Scene Text

For two days, I carried the receipt inside the book.

I told myself it was because I did not want to lose the number. That was true, as far as it went. It was also true that I had begun to recognize the small white rectangle by touch whenever I opened the cover, and that I had not yet found a reason to move it somewhere less temporary.

On the third evening, I finished the book.

The ending did not explain everything. It left one person standing in a doorway, with a decision still unmade, and trusted the reader to understand that the decision mattered more than the answer. I closed the cover and sat for a while, listening to the ordinary sounds of the apartment.

Then I took out the receipt.

I typed a message, deleted it, and typed another. The first sounded as though I had been waiting beside the phone. The second sounded as though I had composed it for an audience. I disliked both versions.

In the end, I wrote: *I finished the book. I think I understand why you hesitated about the ending. I may also have misunderstood it completely.*

Her reply arrived a little later.

*Those are usually the two available options.*

I smiled at the screen.

*Would you be willing to discuss the possibilities over coffee?*

There was a pause long enough for me to put the phone down and pick it up again.

*I would. Thursday? There's a small place on the next street from the old cinema. Four?*

*Four is good.*

*Then try to arrive with an opinion. I dislike doing all the work.*

I read the message twice, though it did not require a second reading. Then I placed the receipt on the table, no longer needing it to keep the number safe.

On Thursday, I arrived seven minutes early.

The café was narrow, with a front window open to the mild afternoon and a row of small tables set close enough together to make private conversations feel gently communal. A ceiling fan turned overhead. Someone behind the counter was arranging cups with the concentration of a person solving a puzzle.

I chose a table near the window and put the book beside my cup.

Maya arrived almost exactly at four.

I saw her pause outside to look at the sign, although there was no real possibility she had come to the wrong place. When she stepped in, her eyes found me. Her expression changed—not dramatically, just enough to make the room feel less like somewhere I had been waiting and more like somewhere we had agreed to be.

“You came prepared,” she said, looking at the book.

“I thought it might be useful evidence.”

“Evidence of what?”

“That I finished it.”

“A surprisingly low standard.”

“I didn't want to begin with an unreasonable promise.”

She sat opposite me. For a moment, we were both aware that this was different. The first time, the rain and the fallen book had supplied us with an opening. Now there was only the fact that we had each chosen to come.

Maya glanced at the book. “So. The ending.”

“I liked it.”

“You sound disappointed.”

“I expected to dislike it. I had prepared a very good argument.”

“That's unfortunate. I was looking forward to hearing it.”

“I can still make the argument. It may be less honest, but it will be well organized.”

She laughed, and the last of the awkwardness loosened.

We ordered coffee. I told her what I thought the final scene meant. She listened without interrupting, her attention resting on me in a way that made me try to be more precise. When I finished, she turned the cup slowly between her hands.

“I read it differently,” she said.

“I suspected you might.”

“I don't think the character was waiting for the right decision. I think she was waiting for someone else to make it easier.”

I looked down at the book.

“That hadn't occurred to me.”

“Does it change your opinion?”

I considered the question. Outside, a bicycle passed the window, its front basket carrying a paper bag that shifted with the uneven pavement.

“It makes the ending less hopeful,” I said.

“Maybe. Or it makes the hope belong to the person reading it.”

I looked up. “That's a rather generous interpretation.”

“I'm not always generous.”

“I'll make a note of that.”

“You should. It may become relevant.”

There was no challenge in her voice, only an invitation to take her seriously. I found that I wanted to.

We talked about the book for a while longer. We disagreed about the character's final silence, about whether a story owed its reader an explanation, and about the habit of remembering a single sentence while forgetting entire chapters. None of it felt like an argument to win. Each time one of us changed the other's mind, the conversation seemed to gain something rather than lose ground.

At one point, Maya asked, “What did you think the character was afraid of?”

I began to answer, then stopped.

“I don't know,” I said. “I think I gave her a reason because I wanted her decision to make sense.”

Maya nodded, as if that answer was more interesting than the one I had been about to give.

“It's tempting,” she said. “To make people easier to understand than they are.”

“Even fictional people?”

“Especially fictional people. They can't correct us.”

I smiled. “You seem to have a complicated relationship with books.”

“I think books are very patient with being misunderstood.”

The afternoon moved around us. Cups were cleared from neighboring tables; the light at the window shifted by a few inches. We eventually left the book closed between us and spoke about smaller things: a shop that had changed its name, the difficulty of finding a quiet place to sit, the peculiar confidence of people who recommended things without asking what you liked.

I learned that Maya preferred walking when she needed to think. She learned that I often waited too long before asking a question. Neither fact was a revelation, but both felt like something carefully placed in the space between us.

When the cups were empty, I glanced at the time.

“We've been here longer than I intended,” I said.

“Is that a complaint?”

“No.”

“Good. I would hate to think the book had wasted your afternoon.”

“It hasn't.”

She looked at me for a moment, then reached for her bag.

Outside, the street was bright but not hot. We walked together as far as the corner. There, the pavement divided around a line of parked scooters, and our routes separated.

“Thank you for coming,” I said.

“I suggested the time and place.”

“Then thank you for suggesting them.”

“That seems fair.”

I wanted to ask when I would see her again. The question was simple, but I could feel myself trying to make it sound effortless. I let the effort go.

“I'd like to do this again,” I said.

Maya looked toward the street, then back at me.

“So would I.”

She said it plainly. No joke to soften it, no promise beyond what the words could hold.

We chose no date. We did not need to turn the afternoon into a schedule before it had finished being an afternoon.

She went one way, and I went the other.

The book was still under my arm. I had brought it as evidence, but I carried it home as something else: a record of how differently two people could read the same pages, and how much there was to discover in the space between them.
