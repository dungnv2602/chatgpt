## Scene Text

The rain began just as I reached the corner.

It came down with the sudden confidence of something that had been waiting all afternoon for permission. One moment the pavement was merely dark with the threat of weather; the next, every passing motorbike was dragging silver water behind it, and the trees were shaking their leaves over the street.

I ducked into the first open door.

A bell gave a small, tired ring above me. The place was narrower than it looked from outside: shelves of books along one wall, a few wooden tables, a counter at the back, and windows already clouding at the edges. It smelled of coffee, damp paper, and the faint sweetness of something baking.

I stood just inside, trying not to bring the whole street in with me.

“It's all right,” someone said. “The floor has survived worse.”

I looked toward the voice.

A woman stood beside the nearest bookshelf, holding a book open in one hand. She had said it without looking up, as if she had been making room for strangers and their weather all her life.

“I'm relieved,” I said. “I was worried I'd be asked to pay for the rain.”

That made her glance up. There was a pause—not quite a smile yet, but the possibility of one.

“They charge extra for thunderstorms,” she said. “This is only the ordinary rate.”

I laughed, and the sound felt unexpectedly loud in the little shop.

I went to the counter, ordered coffee, and chose a table near the window. Outside, the street blurred into moving lights. People hurried past with bags held over their heads. A delivery rider stopped beneath an awning and looked at the sky as though he might negotiate with it.

I had almost settled into the pleasant anonymity of being alone when a book slid from the shelf beside my table and landed face-down on the floor.

I bent to pick it up.

“Careful,” the woman said, approaching. “It has a habit of making a dramatic entrance.”

I turned the book over. “Does it deserve one?”

“Depends on your opinion of the ending.”

I held it out to her. “You're recommending it or warning me?”

“I'm still deciding.”

She took the book, but neither of us immediately returned to what we had been doing. The rain filled the silence between us, steady against the glass.

“What kind of ending makes you hesitate?” I asked.

“The kind that explains everything.” She looked down at the cover, considering it. “Sometimes I think a story should trust you to carry a little uncertainty home.”

“That sounds inconvenient.”

“It is. But so is remembering a story for years.”

I looked at the book again. “Then perhaps the inconvenient ones are the better ones.”

“Perhaps.” She tilted her head. “You say that as if you've been inconvenienced by a book before.”

“I once stayed awake until three in the morning because a fictional person made a poor decision.”

“Only once?”

“I've learned nothing from the experience.”

This time she smiled properly.

It was not a remarkable smile in the theatrical sense. It did not stop the rain or rearrange the furniture or make the room suddenly brighter. It simply made the conversation feel as though it had found the right place to begin.

She told me her name was Maya.

I repeated it once, mostly to make sure I had heard it correctly. Then I told her mine. The exchange was ordinary, but names have a way of changing the atmosphere of a room. A moment earlier she had been the woman by the bookshelf. Now the silence could belong to Maya.

She asked what I was reading. I showed her the book in my bag, and she examined the cover with the seriousness of a doctor considering a difficult case.

“I haven't read this one,” she said.

“That's either encouraging or disappointing.”

“It's too early to decide.”

We talked about books, then about the strange things people underline, then about how a person could spend an entire afternoon searching for the right sentence and still fail to say what they meant. The conversation moved without effort, not because we agreed about everything, but because neither of us seemed to need the other to agree.

At some point, my coffee went cold.

At some point after that, the rain softened.

I noticed both things only when Maya glanced toward the window and said, “I think the weather is letting you go.”

I looked outside. The pavement still shone, but the downpour had become a fine, drifting curtain.

“So it is.”

Neither of us moved.

She closed the book she had been holding and returned it to the shelf. I could have stood, thanked her for the conversation, and walked back into the evening. It would have been the sensible thing. A pleasant encounter, neatly contained. Something to remember without having to risk anything by asking for more.

Instead, I heard myself say, “Would you like to continue this conversation another day?”

Maya looked at me. She did not answer immediately, and I was grateful for that. An instant answer might have made the question seem smaller than it was.

“Another day,” she repeated.

“If the book permits it.”

“The book is notoriously difficult to manage.”

“I had gathered.”

She considered me for another second. Then she took a small receipt from the counter, turned it over, and wrote something on the blank side.

“I think,” she said, handing it to me, “we can arrange something without asking its permission.”

I looked at the number, then back at her.

“Thank you.”

“Don't thank me yet. I haven't chosen the next book.”

“Then I'll have to earn the recommendation.”

“That's a rather ambitious plan for a first meeting.”

“I've had a long afternoon.”

“So have I.”

There was something in the way she said it that made me want to ask what kind of afternoon. I didn't. Not yet. There would be time, if we made some.

I put the receipt carefully inside my book.

At the door, I turned back. Maya had returned to the shelf, but she looked up as the bell rang.

“Try not to get caught in the rain again,” she said.

“I'll do my best.”

“That's not very reassuring.”

“No,” I said. “But it leaves room for a second meeting.”

Her smile followed me out onto the wet pavement.

The rain had almost stopped. The city was carrying on as if nothing had happened, buses breathing at the curb, shopkeepers pulling their awnings back, strangers stepping around puddles without looking at one another.

I walked home with cold coffee still on my tongue and a phone number folded between the pages of a book.

It was a small thing.

I had the feeling, though, that I would remember exactly where I had put it.
