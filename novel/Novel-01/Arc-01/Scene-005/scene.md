## Scene Text

On Tuesday evening, I asked Maya if she would like to walk with me on Saturday.

I wrote the message twice. The first version included an explanation of the route, which made the invitation sound like a guided tour. The second was shorter.

*There's a route I usually take on Saturday mornings. Would you like to walk it with me this weekend?*

Her reply came a few minutes later.

*I'd like that. Is there anything I should know?*

*Comfortable shoes. No dramatic scenery.*

*I can manage one of those.*

I read the exchange again, then put my phone down as if it had asked me something.

On Saturday, she was waiting near the corner where the route began. There was a bus stop, a shuttered repair shop, and a row of narrow buildings whose ground floors had been given over to practical businesses.

“You weren't exaggerating about the scenery,” she said.

“I thought it best to establish expectations.”

“Very considerate.”

We set off. I knew which paving stones shifted underfoot and where the pavement narrowed around a tree. Usually I walked this way alone, without having to decide where to go next.

“Most Saturdays?” she asked.

“Most.”

“What keeps bringing you back?”

I considered the practical answers: it was quiet in the morning, the traffic was manageable, and there was a shop I liked farther along. They were all true. None quite explained it.

“I know the route,” I said. “I don't have to spend the walk deciding where I'm going.”

“That sounds restful.”

“It is. You start noticing other things.”

“Like what?”

“The same window being open. A new plant on a balcony. Whether the bakery has put its chairs outside.”

She looked up at the buildings. “So you notice when something changes because you know what usually stays the same.”

“I hadn't put it that way.”

“Landscape architects are occasionally useful.”

“Occasionally?”

“I'm trying not to overstate my case.”

The stationery shop was halfway down the next street. It was narrow, with shelves of notebooks and envelopes arranged behind the counter. A bell sounded when we entered.

The shopkeeper looked up. “Your usual?”

“Please.”

Maya turned to me. “You have a usual notebook?”

“A usual size.”

“That's not the same thing.”

“It's a distinction of some importance.”

The shopkeeper brought out a plain notebook with a dark cover. I checked the paper, turned a few pages, and nodded. It was uncoated, slightly warm in color, and heavy enough that ink did not show through too easily.

“You write in them?” Maya asked.

“Sometimes.”

“What do you write?”

“Things I don't want to forget.”

She glanced at the notebook, then back at me. “That sounds like a category with very generous boundaries.”

“It has to accommodate grocery lists.”

“And the occasional profound thought?”

“Those are usually written on receipts. I don't want them getting ideas.”

She laughed. I bought the notebook. Outside, Maya paused at the window display and pointed to a small green one.

“That one looks as if it would contain better ideas.”

“An ambitious notebook.”

“Perhaps it has high standards.”

She left it where it was. We continued toward the bakery.

It was not especially attractive, but the bread was good and the tables near the window were usually free before noon. The attendant recognized me with the mild familiarity of someone who had served the same customer often enough to remember an order, but not a name.

“Coffee?” I asked Maya.

“Tea, if they have it.”

“They do.”

At the counter, I hesitated over the pastry case. I usually chose the same small one. Today there was another I had not tried.

Maya noticed. “You can get the unfamiliar one.”

“I wasn't aware there was a test.”

“There isn't. I'm observing.”

“An increasingly demanding profession.”

I chose it. We carried our drinks to a table near the window.

We talked about a book Maya had started and abandoned, a client who had changed their mind about a path after it had already been laid, and a manuscript I had spent most of Thursday trying to persuade to stop explaining itself. She told the story about the path without making the client sound foolish. I liked that about her: she could disagree with someone and still leave them their dignity.

After a while, she looked at the notebook beside my cup.

“Do you bring one everywhere?”

“Not everywhere.”

“Only on important Saturdays?”

“Only when I expect difficult questions.”

“Then I should have prepared.”

“You've done very well.”

She smiled, then watched the street through the window. A delivery rider stopped to check a message. Across the road, someone coaxed a reluctant dog through a doorway.

“It's a good route,” she said.

“Even without dramatic scenery?”

“Especially without it. You know where you're going, but you still leave room to notice what's there.”

I turned the notebook so its edge ran parallel to the table. It was a small adjustment, and not one that improved anything.

“I wanted to show you somewhere I come back to,” I said. “Not because there's anything remarkable about it. It's just part of my week.”

Maya looked at me. She did not rush to fill the pause.

“I like that you showed me,” she said.

There was no clever answer to that. I was relieved not to need one.

When we finished our drinks, I glanced at the time. I could have said I had errands to run. I did have errands, technically. They could wait.

“Would you like to sit a little longer?” I asked.

Maya settled back in her chair. “I would.”

Outside, the dog finally made it through the doorway. I watched it disappear, then turned back to the table. The notebook lay between us, still closed.

For once, I had not waited for someone else to decide where the morning should go.
