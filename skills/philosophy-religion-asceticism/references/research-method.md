# Research method for philosophy, religion, and asceticism

## Philosophy

- Reconstruct an argument before evaluating it: state premises, inferential
  steps, conclusion, and any suppressed assumptions.
- Distinguish an author's own position from later interpretations and from
  textbook shorthand.
- Define contested terms. A term such as “self,” “soul,” “liberation,”
  “nothingness,” “virtue,” or “enlightenment” may have different technical
  meanings across thinkers and periods.
- Present the strongest objection and, where relevant, the strongest reply.
- Separate validity, soundness, conceptual coherence, historical influence,
  and existential appeal. These are different assessments.

## Religious studies and theology

- State whether the analysis is historical-critical, sociological,
  anthropological, philosophical, comparative, or confessional/theological.
- Do not collapse emic accounts (how adherents understand a practice) into
  etic accounts (external scholarly analysis), or vice versa.
- Identify internal diversity: schools, denominations, lineages, regions,
  periods, reform movements, and dissenting voices.
- Treat “orthodoxy,” “heresy,” “canon,” “religion,” and “mysticism” as
  historically situated categories rather than self-evident universal labels.
- When comparing traditions, define a narrow dimension of comparison and
  identify asymmetries in sources, historical context, and institutional power.

## Textual and historical interpretation

- Identify genre: philosophical dialogue, hymn, law code, commentary, myth,
  biography, ritual manual, hagiography, polemic, scripture, or another form.
- Check date, provenance, redaction, authorship, audience, and transmission where
  scholarship makes these relevant.
- Distinguish the earliest recoverable text from later canonical forms,
  commentaries, translations, and modern reconstructions.
- Do not infer universal practice from prescriptive texts alone. Compare with
  historical, archaeological, documentary, and ethnographic evidence where
  possible.
- Quote sparingly and accurately. Explain when translation choices influence
  the interpretation.

## Asceticism and contemplation

Analyse a practice across several dimensions where relevant:

1. **Definition:** What exactly is done, and what is not included?
2. **Aim:** Purification, liberation, salvation, obedience, detachment,
   concentration, solidarity, protest, discipline, or another stated goal?
3. **Theory:** What account of body, desire, mind, suffering, virtue, or spiritual
   progress justifies it within the tradition?
4. **Institution:** Is it individual, communal, monastic, lay, temporary, or
   lifelong? Who authorizes or supervises it?
5. **Historical variation:** How did the practice differ by period, region,
   class, gender, status, and lineage?
6. **Critique:** What internal and external critiques exist? Consider power,
   coercion, social function, gender, health, and access without assuming one
   interpretation in advance.
7. **Evidence of effects:** Separate theological claims, first-person reports,
   historical observations, and modern empirical findings.
8. **Modern adaptation:** Do not assume ancient instructions can be transferred
   safely or faithfully into contemporary life.

## Comparative analysis

Use a comparison matrix only after defining the shared question. Compare like
with like where possible (for example, the role of celibacy in two specified
monastic institutions during comparable periods), and explicitly flag cases
where the categories are not equivalent.

Avoid presenting a superficial list of parallels as proof of borrowing,
identity, or a common essence. Similar practices can emerge for different
reasons. Claims of historical influence require evidence of transmission.

## Handling uncertainty

Use calibrated language:

- **Well established:** supported by strong and convergent evidence.
- **Widely accepted:** common among specialists, with some qualification.
- **Contested:** credible specialist disagreement exists.
- **Plausible:** supported but not demonstrated conclusively.
- **Speculative:** a hypothesis with limited evidence.
- **Unknown / not established:** the evidence does not justify a conclusion.

Do not use these labels as decoration. Use them only when justified by the
literature.
