# Scholarly source standards

## The hierarchy

Use the highest tier appropriate to the claim. A source can be excellent for one
kind of claim and unsuitable for another.

### Tier 1 — Primary evidence

- Critical editions of primary texts, manuscripts, inscriptions, archival
  material, contemporaneous documents, and reliable official documents.
- Established scholarly translations with identified translators, notes, and
  editorial apparatus.
- Official doctrinal or institutional documents when the question is what a
  particular institution currently teaches.

Primary sources establish what a source says or what an institution declares;
they do not automatically establish that its metaphysical claims are true or
that its account of past events is historically accurate.

### Tier 2 — Peer-reviewed specialist scholarship

- Peer-reviewed journal articles and academic monographs by recognized subject
  specialists.
- Critical editions, annotated translations, and research volumes from
  established scholarly publishers.
- University-press publications with transparent scholarly apparatus and
  appropriate editorial review.

Examples of strong academic publishing ecosystems include Oxford University
Press, Cambridge University Press, Harvard University Press, Princeton
University Press, Yale University Press, University of Chicago Press, Columbia
University Press, Cornell University Press, Brill, De Gruyter, and Routledge.
These are starting points, not automatic endorsements of every title.

### Tier 3 — Expert reference works and scholarly syntheses

Use these to establish orientation, terminology, bibliographies, and the state
of a field; follow their references to primary and specialist research for
important claims.

Examples include:

- Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy (SEP).
- Oxford Research Encyclopedias and Oxford Handbooks.
- Cambridge Companions, Cambridge Histories, and Cambridge reference works.
- Brill reference works and specialist encyclopedias.
- Other editorially controlled, expert-authored reference works in the relevant
  field.

A reference entry is not a substitute for a critical edition or the specialist
monograph when the question turns on a disputed detail.

### Tier 4 — Research infrastructure and repositories

Use these to locate or access scholarship and source texts, then cite the
underlying publication or edition whenever possible:

- JSTOR, Project MUSE, publisher platforms, university repositories, and
  institutional libraries.
- WorldCat and national library catalogues for bibliographic verification.
- PhilPapers and discipline-specific bibliographies for discovery.
- Repositories of digitized primary texts when provenance, edition, and
  editorial basis are clear.

A platform hosting a paper is not itself proof that the paper is peer-reviewed.
Check the journal, publisher, author, and publication details.

## Source fit by claim type

| Claim type | Best evidence |
|---|---|
| What a philosopher or scripture says | Critical edition; reputable translation; precise textual locator |
| Historical development | Peer-reviewed historical scholarship; primary evidence; critical historiography |
| Current official doctrine | Current official document from the relevant institution; identify jurisdiction and date |
| What scholars generally think | Recent specialist review, handbook, or multiple peer-reviewed studies; disclose dissent |
| Philosophical argument | Original work plus high-quality contemporary analysis and counterarguments |
| Empirical effect of a practice | Systematic reviews, meta-analyses, high-quality clinical research, and recognized health agencies |
| Lived practice within a community | Ethnography and carefully contextualized first-person sources; do not generalize beyond sample and setting |

## Evaluation checklist

Assess every important source against these questions:

1. **Expertise:** Does the author have demonstrable expertise in the specific
   period, language, tradition, or method?
2. **Publication quality:** Is it peer-reviewed or edited by a credible scholarly
   press or specialist reference project?
3. **Evidence:** Does the work cite primary evidence and engage relevant
   scholarship?
4. **Method:** Are its interpretive, historical, philological, or empirical
   methods appropriate and transparent?
5. **Currency:** Is the publication date adequate for the question? Older classic
   scholarship may remain essential, but should be checked against later work.
6. **Independence:** Do corroborating sources represent genuinely independent
   research rather than repeating one another?
7. **Scope:** Does the source support the exact claim, tradition, time period,
   and population being discussed?
8. **Access:** Was the relevant argument actually inspected, or is only a
   citation, abstract, or snippet available?

## Sources to avoid as evidentiary foundations

Do not use the following as the basis for substantive factual or scholarly
claims:

- SEO content farms, anonymous blogs, unsourced essays, quote aggregators, and
  generic “wisdom” websites.
- Social-media posts, forums, influencer content, or general-purpose videos
  unless the object of study is specifically those media or they are clearly
  labelled as personal testimony rather than scholarship.
- Wikipedia or other open encyclopedias as final authority. They may help with
  discovery, but trace claims to stronger sources.
- Unreviewed lecture notes, AI-generated pages, unattributed translations, or
  copied quotations without provenance.
- Institutional marketing pages when a scholarly or official primary document
  exists.

A popular source may be mentioned as an example of contemporary reception, but
never silently upgraded into academic evidence.

## A note on “top” institutions

There is no universally valid absolute ranking of all institutions across
philosophy, theology, religious studies, philology, history, and clinical
research. Apply the quality criteria above to the specific source and question.
When two strong sources disagree, explain the disagreement rather than choosing
the one with the more prestigious logo.


## High-quality institutional starting points

The following are discovery and verification starting points, not a closed
approved list or a prestige ranking. Select by subject expertise, editorial
method, source quality, and relevance to the exact claim.

### Philosophy and intellectual history
- Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy (editorially reviewed expert entries).
- PhilPapers (bibliographic discovery; verify the underlying publication).
- Oxford University Press, Cambridge University Press, Princeton University
  Press, Harvard University Press, Yale University Press, and University of
  Chicago Press for specialist monographs, companions, and critical editions.

### Religious studies, theology, and textual traditions
- Oxford Research Encyclopedias, Oxford Handbooks, Cambridge Companions and
  Cambridge Histories.
- Brill and De Gruyter for specialist religious-studies, classical, biblical,
  Islamic, Buddhist, and philological scholarship.
- Society of Biblical Literature publications and peer-reviewed journals
  relevant to biblical studies.
- Relevant national libraries, manuscript libraries, and university-based
  textual projects for provenance-aware primary materials.
- Official bodies of a tradition only when establishing that body's own
  current teaching, practice, or institutional position; do not treat them as
  neutral arbiters of historical or comparative claims.

### History, languages, and primary-text research
- University libraries and established manuscript collections.
- Perseus Digital Library and comparable scholarly text projects, while
  checking the edition and editorial provenance of each text.
- Loeb Classical Library and other established critical/annotated series where
  appropriate; identify the exact edition and translation used.
- WorldCat and national library catalogues for bibliographic verification,
  not as evidence for the content of a work.

### Empirical and health-related claims
- Cochrane reviews, recognized national public-health agencies, and
  peer-reviewed medical journals for claims about fasting, sleep deprivation,
  nutrition, mental health, or physiological effects.
- Use clinical guidelines and systematic reviews where available. Do not
  extrapolate findings from one population, intervention, or duration to
  another.

These examples do not imply that every item from these organizations is
peer-reviewed, current, methodologically sound, or appropriate for every
question. Inspect the individual work and its editorial process.

## Mandatory source audit

Before citing a source, confirm as far as access permits:
- the exact title, author/editor, publication venue, and date;
- the author's relevant subject expertise;
- the publication's peer-review or editorial process;
- the edition, translator, and textual locator for primary sources;
- that the source actually supports the sentence being cited;
- whether the source is primary evidence, institutional self-description,
  specialist interpretation, empirical research, or a discovery tool.

If any item cannot be checked, qualify the citation and disclose the limitation.

## Evaluating books for a scholarly reading list or collection

When assessing a proposed addition, do not collapse publisher prestige, book
quality, and collection value into one judgment. Evaluate them separately:

- **Institutional and publisher standing:** Verify the publisher/imprint and
  distinguish university presses from commercial academic publishers. Verify
  editor/author affiliations independently; an author's university is not the
  book's publisher. Prestige is contextual, not a quality guarantee.
- **The individual work:** Examine its editors and contributors, relevant
  expertise, editorial design, methods, primary-source engagement, bibliography,
  publication/review process, and independent scholarly reception. A publisher's
  general procedures do not prove that every chapter was independently
  peer-reviewed.
- **Marginal value:** Compare the title with the user's existing books. Identify
  direct overlap, genuinely new arguments or coverage, missing topics, depth
  relative to specialist alternatives, and whether it fills a real research gap.
  Conclude whether it is essential, valuable but optional, or redundant/low
  priority, with reasons.

Use publisher catalogues and institutional repositories for bibliographic facts,
the book or its table of contents for scope, and independent scholarly reviews
for critical reception. Goodreads and retailer pages may help identify a title
but are not evidence of scholarly merit. If the full text or review process is
inaccessible, disclose that limitation rather than guessing.
