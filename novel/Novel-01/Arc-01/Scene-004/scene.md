- **Dramatic or Emotional Focus:** The difference between showing someone a finished result and allowing them to see what remains unfinished, uncertain, or beyond one's control.

## 4. Opening Situation

- Character positions and relationship state: Interested acquaintances who have begun choosing to spend time together.
- Immediate context: This is a place Maya knows well, but she has not previously shown it to someone she is getting to know.

## 5. Scene Development
- Main actions and interactions: Maya guides the narrator through the courtyard, pointing out details of its original design and the changes made by residents. A conversation with a resident briefly interrupts their walk.
- Dialogue and important exchanges: Maya explains the decisions behind the space without turning the visit into a formal presentation. The narrator asks questions about what she would change now; her answer is more complicated than he expects.
- Emotional developments: Maya allows him to see both her pride in the work and her uncertainty about parts of it. The narrator resists the impulse to turn her uncertainty into reassurance.
- New information or revelations: Maya values the ways people make a designed place their own, even when those changes complicate the original plan.

## 6. Central Moment
- **Key Action, Revelation, Decision, or Turning Point:** Maya notices that a resident has moved and repurposed part of the courtyard furniture. Rather than correcting it, she asks the resident how the arrangement works for them.
- **Why It Matters:** The moment reveals that Maya's openness to adaptation is not merely an attractive professional idea; it is a practice she has to keep choosing.

## 7. Ending
- How the scene concludes: They sit in the altered arrangement for a while. Maya admits there are parts of the courtyard she would design differently now, and the narrator lets the admission stand without trying to improve it.
- What changes: Maya has shared something she cares about without presenting it as flawless.

## Scene Text

Maya sent me the address on Thursday evening.

*Saturday, if you're still interested. The courtyard is easiest to find from the side entrance.*

I replied that I was.

On Saturday, I found her standing beside a narrow gate, looking down at her phone. She put it away when she saw me.

“You came,” she said.

“You invited me.”

“I did. I wasn't sure whether I'd made it sound like an assignment.”

“Should I have brought a notebook?”

“Only if you promise not to take minutes.”

The gate opened onto a rectangular courtyard enclosed by the walls of a residential building. It was larger than I had expected, though the space had been divided into smaller areas: a paved section near the entrance, a few planted beds, and a shaded corner with tables and chairs. A tree stood near the middle, its branches reaching over the upper floors.

At first glance, it seemed orderly. Then I noticed the evidence of use. One chair had been turned toward the tree. A small collection of pots occupied a corner beside a drain. Someone had placed a folded blanket over the back of a bench.

“This is the place?” I asked.

“This is it.”

“It looks different from the garden.”

“It should. People have to live around this one.”

She said it lightly, but she was watching the courtyard as we entered, taking in the chairs and the plants with an expression I couldn't quite read.

“Did you design all of it?”

“I worked on the landscape plan. Other people had a hand in the building and the rest of the project.” She pointed toward the planted beds. “Those were meant to give the windows some privacy without closing the space in.”

“Do they?”

“Mostly. The plants grew faster than the maintenance schedule anticipated.”

“An occupational hazard?”

“A predictable one, apparently.”

She showed me where the paths had been intended to run and where people now walked instead. The difference was not dramatic. A narrow strip of grass had become a worn track between two paved areas. The chairs had migrated toward the shade. A low table had been moved close to the wall, where someone had left a newspaper folded beneath a mug.

“Does it bother you?” I asked.

“Which part?”

“That the plan has been changed.”

She considered the question. “I used to think it meant I hadn't thought something through. Sometimes it does. But sometimes people are answering a question the plan couldn't ask.”

“What question is that?”

“Where do you actually want to sit? What do you need when you come downstairs? How much sun is pleasant before it becomes too much?”

A woman came through the side entrance carrying a bag of groceries. She greeted Maya and glanced at me.

“This is the designer,” she said, with the ease of someone identifying a familiar neighbor.

“Part of the design team,” Maya corrected.

The woman pointed toward the chairs beneath the tree. “We moved those again. The other arrangement was too hot in the afternoon.”

“I noticed,” Maya said.

“It's much better now.”

“Good. I'll have a look.”

The woman continued toward the building. Maya watched her go, then turned to me.

“I was going to ask her about that.”

“You could have.”

“I could have. But she seems to have solved it.”

We walked toward the chairs. The arrangement was not symmetrical. One chair faced the entrance, another the tree; a third had been pulled close enough to a small table that someone could reach it without standing. It looked less designed than assembled through repeated use.

“Would you change anything?” I asked.

“Several things.”

She said it without hesitation, then looked at the space again.

“But not those chairs,” she added.

“Because they work?”

“Because people have decided they work. That's useful information.”

She sat in the chair nearest the tree and moved it a few inches to make room for me. I chose the one beside her. From there, the courtyard felt quieter. A window opened somewhere above us; a voice called to someone inside. A scooter passed beyond the gate and faded into the street.

“Do you like coming here?” I asked.

“I do. I like seeing what happens after the drawings are finished.”

“Is that when the work is finished?”

She looked at me.

“Officially, sometimes.”

“And unofficially?”

“I'm not sure it ever is.”

She rested one hand on the arm of the chair. There was a small scratch in the wood, pale against the darker finish.

“There are things I'd do differently now,” she said. “The drainage near that corner, for one. And I would have argued harder for a little more shade.”

“Would you call it a mistake?”

“Some of it. Not all of it.” She gave a short laugh. “I'm trying to learn that those aren't the same thing.”

I had an answer ready. It was the sort of answer I often gave when someone admitted uncertainty: measured, reassuring, and designed to make the discomfort smaller. I stopped before saying it.

Maya glanced at me. “What?”

“Nothing. I was about to tell you that every project teaches you something.”

“That sounds like something printed on a conference tote bag.”

“It does.”

“I'm glad you didn't say it.”

“I thought better of it.”

She smiled, not as though I had said something particularly clever, but as though she appreciated that I had left the sentence unsaid.

We stayed there while the light shifted across the paving. A child came out with a small plastic watering can and began tending to the pots near the drain. The water missed most of the soil. The child seemed unconcerned.

“Those aren't part of the original planting,” Maya said.

“Are you going to tell them?”

“No.” She watched the watering can tip again. “I think those belong to someone here.”

“Then perhaps they're exactly where they should be.”

“Perhaps.”

She didn't sound entirely convinced, but she didn't get up.

After a while, she asked what I thought of the courtyard.

I looked around before answering. I could have said it was pleasant, or that the tree made the space feel less enclosed. Both were true, but neither seemed to be what she was asking.

“It feels used,” I said. “Not neglected. Just… lived in.”

Maya looked toward the chairs.

“I'll take that.”

“Was there a correct answer?”

“No. But there were several answers I was prepared to disagree with.”

“I'm relieved to have avoided them.”

She laughed. Then she leaned back, letting the conversation rest.

When we eventually stood, she walked me back to the gate. At the entrance, she paused to straighten a small sign that had tilted against the wall.

“Thank you for coming,” she said.

“Thank you for showing me.”

“I wasn't sure whether it would be interesting.”

“It was.”

She nodded, accepting the answer without asking me to explain it.

We parted at the corner. I watched her return through the gate, then continued down the street.

I thought about the chair she had left where someone else had put it, and about the sentence she had begun without knowing exactly how it would end. I had not tried to finish it for her. It was a small restraint, but it felt different from silence.

For once, I had let a moment remain unfinished.
